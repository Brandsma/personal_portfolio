Research has always compounded. Newton stood on the shoulders of giants; every generation of giants stands on more giants. The question is whether something qualitatively different is happening now, or whether we're just bad at appreciating the rate of prior eras.

I think something qualitatively different is happening. The argument isn't that research is faster — it's that the *tooling for doing research* is itself getting faster at a rate that wasn't previously the case.

## what's actually speeding up

The obvious one: literature review. A task that used to take weeks — getting oriented in a new subfield, finding the five key papers, tracing the lineage — now takes hours with the right tools. This is real and not nothing.

Less obvious: the distance from "idea" to "first experiment" has collapsed. A researcher who would have spent two weeks implementing a baseline can now spend two days. The experiments themselves haven't gotten faster, but the overhead around them has.

Least appreciated: writing and communication. Papers are getting written faster, conference reviewing is getting harder to do well, and the pressure to publish is increasing in proportion.

## what isn't speeding up

Insight. The moment where you look at the results and understand what they mean. This is still as slow as it ever was. Maybe slower, because there are more results to look at.

Trust. The process by which a finding becomes something a field actually believes and acts on. Faster publication hasn't shortened this; if anything, the noise has lengthened it.

## what we lose

The thing I worry about is mistaking the acceleration of *throughput* for the acceleration of *understanding*. A paper pipeline that takes ideas from hypothesis to preprint in a month is impressive. A research culture that mistakes that throughput for progress is dangerous.

There's no clean answer here. The tools are good and I use them. I just try to remember that the point isn't to move fast — it's to be right.
