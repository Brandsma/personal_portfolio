Invertible neural networks are seductive on paper. You get exact likelihoods, you get free inversion, the math is elegant, and the figures are clean. Then you try to use them to solve a real inverse problem and you discover an entire layer of folklore that lives mostly in GitHub issues and the offhand remarks of people three coffees into a workshop.

This is an unsorted dump of the things I had to learn the hard way.

## 1. coupling layers are not interchangeable

Real-NVP, Glow, and the various invertible ResNets all "work," but they fail in characteristically different ways. Real-NVP is the most forgiving to train but expressively limited; Glow is more powerful but extremely sensitive to the exact 1×1 conv parametrisation; iResNets are beautiful but a pain to make stable at the depths you actually need.

For my problem (recovering velocity fields from sparse pressure measurements) I ended up with a hybrid: Real-NVP for the bulk of the depth and a small Glow head at the top.

## 2. the jacobian-determinant term is the entire game

> If your training looks fine but your samples look like garbage, the determinant term is silently dominating and you don't know it.

Half my early bugs came from not logging the magnitude of the log-det separately from the data term. Once I split them, the failure modes became diagnosable in minutes instead of days.

## 3. preconditioning the input matters more than the architecture

This is the unglamorous one. A non-trivial fraction of "this architecture doesn't work" is actually "your input distribution is so badly conditioned that no architecture would work." Whitening, log-transforming, clipping outliers, and choosing the right reference frame routinely outperformed any architectural change I tried.

## 4. physics priors aren't optional in low-data regimes

I have ~10⁴ training trajectories, which sounds like a lot until you remember that the underlying PDE has degrees of freedom in the millions. Soft constraints — penalising violations of incompressibility, of symmetry, of monotonicity in the right places — turned the problem from "doesn't generalise at all" to "generalises about as well as a good classical method, but with calibrated uncertainty."

## 5. nobody actually knows how to evaluate these

Likelihood numbers are seductive and largely meaningless across architectures. Sample quality is qualitative. Held-out reconstruction error tells you about reconstruction, not about the underlying posterior. I now report at least four different metrics and make peace with the fact that none of them are "the" metric.
