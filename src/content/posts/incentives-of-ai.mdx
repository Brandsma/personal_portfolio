The AI field is full of people who are smart, well-intentioned, and subject to a set of incentive structures that reliably produce outcomes none of them would individually endorse.

This is not a scandal. This is just how incentives work. But it seems worth being explicit about the structure, because most of the public discourse about AI safety and AI risk spends its energy on the *intentions* of the people involved rather than the *systems* they're embedded in.

## who's pulling on which lever

**Labs** are in a race they can't individually exit. The logic is clear: if your competitor is going to build powerful AI anyway, you'd rather be the one doing it carefully than cede the ground to someone less careful. This logic is individually rational and collectively produces a race to the bottom on timelines.

**Investors** want returns on a horizon that doesn't intersect with most safety concerns. A model that generates impressive demos in 2026 is valuable even if its long-run effects are complicated. The incentive is to fund capability, not caution.

**Researchers** are measured by papers, and papers are easier to publish when they show improvements. "We tried X and it didn't work, and here's what that tells us" is harder to publish than "we tried X and it worked better." The incentive is to show capability.

**Governments** are slow-moving institutions trying to regulate something that moves faster than legislation. The incentive is to look like you're doing something without actually constraining anything that would move abroad.

## why the levers don't move what they're labelled to move

The "safety" teams at labs are often doing genuinely useful work. They are also, structurally, inside an organisation whose survival depends on shipping products. The incentive is to define "safe enough to ship" in a way that permits shipping.

The "alignment" research community is producing interesting theoretical work. It is also, structurally, a community whose continued funding depends on the problem remaining unsolved. The incentive is to make progress visible without making it conclusive.

I don't think the individuals in these systems are bad actors. I think they're responding rationally to the incentives they're in. The question is whether the systems can be changed — and who would have to give up what to change them.
