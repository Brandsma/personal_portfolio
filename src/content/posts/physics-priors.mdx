Every few months someone publishes a beautiful paper showing a neural network learning to simulate a PDE. The figures are stunning. The error metrics are impressive. The follow-up emails from people trying to use the method on their actual problem are uniformly disappointed.

## why it doesn't generalise

The honest answer is that the model has memorised a manifold of "things that look like training data" and any departure from that manifold — a different boundary condition, a slightly different forcing — sends the prediction off the rails. This is fine for impressive demos. It is not fine for engineers who need to trust the surrogate at design time.

## physics priors as guardrails

The fix that actually works is to bake conservation laws and symmetries directly into the architecture or loss. Not as soft penalties added at the end, but as *structural* constraints the network cannot easily violate.

- Divergence-free outputs for incompressible flows (don't let the model learn it; enforce it).
- Energy-conserving updates for Hamiltonian systems.
- Equivariance under the symmetries of the underlying physics.

## cost vs. benefit

Yes, this makes the architecture harder to write. Yes, the demo numbers will sometimes look slightly worse on the training distribution. The tradeoff you're getting is: the model fails *safely* off-distribution, in ways that physically make sense. That's the difference between a research artefact and a tool an engineer will sign off on.
