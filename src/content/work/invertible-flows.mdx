A project where I spent the better part of a year researching fish, physics and novel neural network architectures. The context is as follows: a small sphere moves silently through the water while an array of eight sensors sits motionless on the bottom, quietly listening. Not listening for sound, but for something far more subtle: the movement of water itself as it flows around the moving object.

This scene captures the essence of **[hydrodynamic imaging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrodynamics)** – the art of "seeing" underwater using only the patterns of water movement. It's an (evolutionary) skill that fish have mastered over millions of years through their [lateral line organs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lateral_line), and one that we're now trying to teach machines.

The experimental setup is deceptively simple. Eight optical sensors are arranged in a neat line across the bottom of our virtual pool, each one 50 millimeters apart, creating a 400-millimeter sensing array. Above them, a sphere travels various paths – sometimes straight, sometimes curved, sometimes in complex zigzag patterns that would make a drunk fish proud.

As the sphere moves, it creates predictable disturbances in the water that follow the elegant equations of **[potential flow theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potential_flow)**. Every movement generates a unique signature in the flow field – a hydrodynamic fingerprint that our sensors can detect.

## Understanding Potential Flow: The Invisible Architecture of Moving Water

Before diving into the mathematics, it's worth understanding what [potential flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potential_flow) actually represents. Think of water as a three-dimensional grid of tiny particles, all connected by invisible springs. When our sphere moves through this grid, it doesn't just bulldoze water out of the way – it creates a coordinated dance where every water particle adjusts its motion to accommodate the sphere's presence.

This adjustment follows strict rules. Water can't be compressed (it's incompressible), water particles can't suddenly start spinning (the flow is irrotational), and there's no friction to speak of (it's inviscid). These constraints create surprisingly elegant patterns. Picture dropping a stone into a perfectly still pond – the ripples spread outward in perfect circles because the water is following mathematical laws, not random chance.

In three dimensions around our moving sphere, the flow pattern looks like streamlines wrapping around the object, squeezing together where the sphere is widest and spreading apart where it tapers. These streamlines carry information – their density tells us about flow speed, their direction reveals the sphere's motion, and their overall pattern encodes the sphere's size.

> ⚡ **Key Insight**: Fish don't measure water pressure directly. They measure how the flow pattern deforms around moving objects. It's like reading the shadow an object casts in water flow instead of seeing the object itself.

When our sphere glides through the pool, it doesn't just push water out of the way randomly. Instead, it creates patterns governed by mathematical laws that have been understood for centuries. The beauty starts with the **[velocity potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Velocity_potential)** – a scalar function φ(x,y,z) that completely describes how water wants to flow around our moving sphere.

For [incompressible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incompressible_flow), [irrotational flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irrotational_vector_field) (which is a reasonable approximation for our sensing scenario), we can define the velocity field as the negative gradient of this potential:

$$
\vec{v} = -\nabla \phi = -[\partial\phi/\partial x, \partial\phi/\partial y, \partial\phi/\partial z]
$$

This means if we know φ everywhere, we know the entire flow field. For a sphere of radius _a_ moving with velocity $\vec{W}$ through an inviscid fluid, the velocity potential becomes:

$$
\phi(x,y,z) = (a^3/2) \times \frac{(\vec{W} \cdot \vec{r})}{ \lvert\vec{r}\rvert^3}
$$


where $\vec{r}$ = [x,y,z] is the position vector from the sphere's center. This equation is remarkably elegant – it says that the flow disturbance depends on the cube of the sphere size ($a^3$), scales linearly with velocity ($\vec{W}$), and falls off as the cube of distance ($\vert\vec{r}\vert^3$).

But here's where the mathematics show us something insightful. This velocity potential must satisfy **[Laplace's equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laplace%27s_equation)**:

$$
\nabla^2\phi = \frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial y^2} + \frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial z^2} = 0
$$

This is a fundamental physical constraint that any realistic flow must satisfy. The flows we detect are solutions to this [partial differential equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_differential_equation), which means they carry the fingerprints of the underlying physics.

For our sensor array positioned along the x-axis, we can derive the specific velocity components that our sensors detect. After some calculus, the flow patterns decompose into three fundamental "wavelets":

```python
def compute_wavelets(rho):
    """
    Compute the three fundamental wavelets for lateral line sensing
    
    Args:
        rho: normalized sensor position (s-x)/y where s is sensor position,
             (x,y) is sphere position
    
    Returns:
        even_wavelet, odd_wavelet, navelet
    """
    denominator = (1 + rho**2)**(5/2)
    
    even_wavelet = (1 - 2*rho**2) / denominator      # Ψₑ: parallel motion
    odd_wavelet = -3*rho / denominator               # Ψₒ: perpendicular motion  
    navelet = (2 - rho**2) / denominator             # Ψₙ: directional info
    
    return even_wavelet, odd_wavelet, navelet
```

These wavelets are the mathematical building blocks of hydrodynamic sensing. The **even wavelet** captures signatures of parallel motion, the **odd wavelet** captures perpendicular motion, and the **navelet** provides additional directional information.

The velocity components our sensors actually measure combine these wavelets in specific ways:

$$
v_x = \frac{a^3 \left\lVert w \right\rVert}{2d^3}(\Psi_o \sin{\theta} - \Psi_e \cos{\theta}),
$$
$$
v_y = \frac{a^3 \left\lVert w \right\rVert}{2d^3}(\Psi_n \sin{\theta} + \Psi_o \cos{\theta}).
$$



Here, $d$ is the distance from sphere to sensor array, and $\theta$ is the angle of motion. Notice how the sphere size appears cubed ($a^3$) – this means a sphere twice as large creates eight times the signal. The distance also appears cubed in the denominator, so doubling the distance reduces the signal by a factor of eight. This cubic relationship makes detection of small, distant objects extraordinarily challenging.

```python
def compute_velocity_profiles(sphere_pos, sphere_velocity, sphere_radius, 
                            sensor_positions, angle):
    """
    Compute velocity profiles at sensor locations for a moving sphere
    """
    x, y = sphere_pos
    sensors = np.array(sensor_positions)
    
    # Compute normalized positions for each sensor
    rho = (sensors - x) / y
    distance = np.sqrt(x**2 + y**2)
    
    # Get wavelets for all sensor positions
    psi_e, psi_o, psi_n = compute_wavelets(rho)
    
    # Velocity magnitude scaling factor
    velocity_magnitude = np.linalg.norm(sphere_velocity)
    scale_factor = (sphere_radius**3 * velocity_magnitude) / (2 * distance**3)
    
    # Compute velocity components
    v_x = scale_factor * (psi_o * np.sin(angle) - psi_e * np.cos(angle))
    v_y = scale_factor * (psi_n * np.sin(angle) + psi_o * np.cos(angle))
    
    return v_x, v_y
```

These mathematical relationships connect the physical world of moving spheres to sensor measurements through well-understood equations. Fish have been using these patterns for millions of years. Now we're teaching machines to decode them.

So what can we actually extract from these hydrodynamic signatures? Traditional research in this field focused on two basic pieces of information – where the object is (x, y coordinates) and which way it's heading (angle $\theta$). But looking at our equations, we should be able to extract much more. The velocity potential contains information about the sphere's size ($a^3$ term) and speed ($\lVert\vec{W}\rVert$ term). The question was: could we actually recover these hidden characteristics from real sensor data?

This became one of the central challenges of my research. Previous work had established that location and angle were recoverable, but speed and size had never been successfully extracted from artificial lateral line data before. The mathematical relationships were clear, but the [inverse problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse_problem) – working backwards from noisy sensor measurements to object properties – proved incredibly challenging.

Working backwards from sensor readings to object properties is what mathematicians call an **inverse problem**. It's notoriously difficult because it's often [ill-posed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Well-posed_problem) – meaning multiple solutions can exist, or small changes in measurements can lead to wildly different answers. In our case, consider the equation:

$$
signal\ strength \propto (a^3 \times \lVert\vec{W}\rVert) / d^3
$$

A small, fast sphere close to the sensors (small $a$, large $\lVert\vec{W}\rVert$, small $d$) might create exactly the same signal as a large, slow sphere further away (large a, small $\lVert\vec{W}\rVert$, large $d$). This is the fundamental ambiguity that makes inverse problems so challenging.

> ⚠️ **The Fundamental Challenge**: Inverse problems are like trying to figure out what someone looks like from their shadow. Multiple different shapes can cast very similar shadows, especially when the shadow is blurry or distorted.

![ill-posed-forward-v-backward.png](https://i.postimg.cc/RF5qNqyh/ill-posed-forward-v-backward.png)

Real sensors add another layer of complexity – noise. Our sensors don't measure perfect velocities; they measure velocities plus random errors:

$$
v_\text{measured} = v_\text{true} + n\
\text{where}\ n \sim N(0, σ²)
$$

The [signal-to-noise ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signal-to-noise_ratio) (SNR) becomes:

$$
SNR = \frac{\lvert v_\text{true}\rvert}{\sigma} = \frac{(a^3 \times \lVert\vec{W}\rVert \times \text{wavelet amplitude})}{ (d^3 \times \sigma)}
$$

This means small, slow, or distant objects become exponentially harder to detect and characterize. When SNR drops below about 3, reliable detection becomes nearly impossible.

Instead of trying to solve the inverse problem analytically, we can train [neural networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neural_network) to learn the complex mapping from sensor data to object properties directly from examples. Previous research had established that **[Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_short-term_memory)** networks excel at this task, and the key insight is that temporal context is everything.

While a single snapshot of sensor data might be ambiguous, watching how the signals evolve over time provides crucial additional information. LSTMs are a specialized type of [recurrent neural network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recurrent_neural_network) designed to capture long-term dependencies in sequential data through sophisticated gating mechanisms that control information flow and memory retention. For a detailed explanation of LSTM architecture and functionality, see the [Wikipedia article on LSTMs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_short-term_memory).

```python
class LateralLineLSTM(tf.keras.Model):
    def __init__(self, hidden_units=256, window_size=16):
        super().__init__()
        self.window_size = window_size
        self.lstm = tf.keras.layers.LSTM(
            hidden_units, 
            activation='tanh',
            return_sequences=False
        )
        self.dense = tf.keras.layers.Dense(3)  # x, y, theta
        
    def call(self, inputs):
        # inputs shape: (batch_size, time_steps, features)
        # features = 16 (8 sensors × 2 velocity components)
        lstm_out = self.lstm(inputs)
        predictions = self.dense(lstm_out)
        return predictions
```

Think of it like this: if you hear a single musical note, you might not recognize the song. But hearing that note in the context of the notes before and after makes the melody clear. The LSTM does the same thing with hydrodynamic signatures.

But I wondered if we could do better with more sophisticated architectures. My first innovation involved **[Invertible Neural Networks (INNs)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.04730)** – a fascinating class of networks that can learn bidirectional mappings. Unlike traditional networks that only learn X → Y, INNs learn both X → Y AND Y → X simultaneously.

This works through "[coupling layers](https://arxiv.org/abs/1605.08803)" that implement a specific mathematical structure. For an input vector x split into two parts x₁ and x₂:

```python
def coupling_layer_forward(x1, x2, scale_net, translation_net):
    """
    Forward pass through a coupling layer
    """
    s = scale_net(x1)      # Scale parameters
    t = translation_net(x1)  # Translation parameters
    
    y1 = x1                           # Pass through unchanged
    y2 = x2 * tf.exp(s) + t          # Affine transformation
    
    return y1, y2

def coupling_layer_inverse(y1, y2, scale_net, translation_net):
    """
    Inverse pass through the same coupling layer
    """
    s = scale_net(y1)      # Same scale network
    t = translation_net(y1)  # Same translation network
    
    x1 = y1                           # Direct recovery
    x2 = (y2 - t) * tf.exp(-s)       # Exact inverse
    
    return x1, x2
```

> 🔄 **The Invertibility Magic**: Half of the input passes through unchanged while the other half undergoes a carefully designed transformation. Because we know exactly how the transformation was applied, we can always undo it perfectly.

Half of the input passes through unchanged, while the other half undergoes an [affine transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_transformation) (scaling and translation) based on the unchanged half. Because affine transformations are easily invertible, we can always recover the original input exactly.

The [Jacobian determinant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobian_matrix_and_determinant) (needed for proper probability calculations) has a simple form:

$$
\lvert \text{det}(\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}) \rvert = \exp(\sum_i s_i)
$$

where sᵢ are the outputs of the scale network. This exponential form is both easy to compute and numerically stable.

For our lateral line problem, we can train the network to learn the mapping from object properties (x, y, $\theta$) to sensor readings ($v_x$, $v_y$), and we automatically get the inverse mapping for free:

```python
class LateralLineINN(tf.keras.Model):
    def __init__(self, num_coupling_layers=4, hidden_dims=64):
        super().__init__()
        self.coupling_layers = []
        
        for i in range(num_coupling_layers):
            # Each coupling layer has its own scale and translation networks
            scale_net = tf.keras.Sequential([
                tf.keras.layers.Dense(hidden_dims, activation='relu'),
                tf.keras.layers.Dense(hidden_dims, activation='relu'),
                tf.keras.layers.Dense(hidden_dims, activation='relu'),
                tf.keras.layers.Dense(hidden_dims, activation='relu'),
                tf.keras.layers.Dense(16)  # Output dim for half the features
            ])
            
            translation_net = tf.keras.Sequential([
                tf.keras.layers.Dense(hidden_dims, activation='relu'),
                tf.keras.layers.Dense(hidden_dims, activation='relu'),
                tf.keras.layers.Dense(hidden_dims, activation='relu'),
                tf.keras.layers.Dense(hidden_dims, activation='relu'),
                tf.keras.layers.Dense(16)  # Output dim for half the features
            ])
            
            self.coupling_layers.append((scale_net, translation_net))
    
    def forward(self, x):
        # x: object properties [x, y, theta] + zero padding
        for scale_net, translation_net in self.coupling_layers:
            x = self.coupling_step_forward(x, scale_net, translation_net)
            x = self.permute(x)  # Permute dimensions between layers
        return x  # sensor readings [v_x, v_y] + latent variables
    
    def inverse(self, y):
        # y: sensor readings + latent variables
        for scale_net, translation_net in reversed(self.coupling_layers):
            y = self.permute_inverse(y)
            y = self.coupling_step_inverse(y, scale_net, translation_net)
        return y  # object properties
```

But there's a subtlety here. Real inverse problems are often ill-posed, meaning multiple inputs can produce the same output. INNs handle this by augmenting the output space with latent variables $z$ sampled from a known distribution:

$$
\text{Forward}: (x, y, \theta) + \text{zero padding} \rightarrow (v_x, v_y) + \text{latent variables}
$$
$$
\text{Inverse}: (v_x, v_y) + \text{latent variables} \rightarrow (x, y, \theta) + \text{recovered padding}
$$


The latent variables capture the "missing information" needed to make the inverse mapping unique. During training, we ensure these latent variables follow a standard [Gaussian distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_distribution) using [Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kernel_embedding_of_distributions#Maximum_mean_discrepancy) loss.

The second innovation drew from **[Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics-informed_neural_networks)**. The core idea is elegant: if we know the physical laws governing our system, why not incorporate them directly into the learning process?

Remember our Laplace equation constraint? For potential flow around a sphere, this translates to a specific relationship between the measured velocities. Starting from the velocity potential and taking the required derivatives, we get:

$$
\frac{\partial v_x}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial v_y}{\partial y} + \frac{3(W_xx + W_yy)}{(x^2 + y^2)^{(5/2)}} = 0
$$

![PINN-schematic.png](https://i.postimg.cc/Nj2gmB1H/PINN-schematic.png)
The image shows a Physics-informed neural network where on the left side, we make use of a "regular" neural network. On the right side we then enhance it with the error that derives from a partial differential equation. This physical law becomes an additional "loss term" during training:

```python
def physics_loss(x, y, v_x, v_y, W_x, W_y):
    """
    Compute the physics-informed loss based on Laplace equation
    """
    # Use automatic differentiation to compute partial derivatives
    with tf.GradientTape() as tape:
        tape.watch([x, y])
        # Predict velocities from current position
        v_x_pred, v_y_pred = model(tf.stack([x, y], axis=1))
    
    # Compute gradients
    dvx_dx = tape.gradient(v_x_pred, x)
    dvy_dy = tape.gradient(v_y_pred, y)
    
    # Physics constraint from Laplace equation
    distance_squared = x**2 + y**2
    physics_term = 3 * (W_x * x + W_y * y) / (distance_squared**(5/2))
    
    # Residual should be zero for physically consistent flows
    residual = dvx_dx + dvy_dy + physics_term
    
    return tf.reduce_mean(tf.square(residual))
```

> 🧠 **Physics as Teacher**: Instead of just learning from examples, the network also learns from the fundamental laws of physics. It's like having both a tutor and a textbook teaching the same subject.

The network has to not only fit the training data but also respect the laws of fluid dynamics. It's like teaching a student not just to memorize facts, but to understand the underlying principles.

I combined both innovations into a novel architecture: **Physics-Informed Invertible Neural Networks (PI/INNs)**. The key insight was that traditional PINNs can only enforce physics on the forward direction. But what if we want physics-informed inverse mappings? The INN architecture solved this elegantly: by training on the forward direction with physics constraints, we automatically get a physics-informed inverse mapping for (semi) free!

The complete loss function balances multiple objectives:

```python
def total_loss(x_true, y_true, theta_true, v_x_true, v_y_true):
    """
    Combined loss function for PI/INN
    """
    # Forward pass: (x,y,θ) → (v_x,v_y,z)
    forward_output = model.forward(tf.stack([x_true, y_true, theta_true], axis=1))
    v_x_pred, v_y_pred, z_pred = tf.split(forward_output, [8, 8, 32], axis=1)
    
    # Backward pass: (v_x,v_y,z) → (x,y,θ)
    backward_input = tf.concat([v_x_true, v_y_true, z_pred], axis=1)
    backward_output = model.inverse(backward_input)
    x_recon, y_recon, theta_recon = tf.split(backward_output, [1, 1, 1], axis=1)
    
    # Data fitting losses
    forward_loss = tf.reduce_mean(tf.square(v_x_pred - v_x_true) + 
                                 tf.square(v_y_pred - v_y_true))
    backward_loss = tf.reduce_mean(tf.square(x_recon - x_true) + 
                                  tf.square(y_recon - y_true) + 
                                  tf.square(theta_recon - theta_true))
    
    # Physics constraint loss
    physics_loss_val = physics_loss(x_true, y_true, v_x_pred, v_y_pred, 
                                   W_x_true, W_y_true)
    
    # Latent distribution loss (MMD to enforce Gaussian distribution)
    z_target = tf.random.normal(tf.shape(z_pred))
    latent_loss = maximum_mean_discrepancy(z_pred, z_target)
    
    return forward_loss + backward_loss + 0.1 * physics_loss_val + 0.01 * latent_loss
```

This creates a network that simultaneously learns accurate mappings, respects physical laws, and handles the ill-posed nature of inverse problems.

With the different neural architectures ready, it was time to put them to the test. I created a comprehensive dataset with spheres of different sizes (10-50mm) moving at different speeds (10-50mm/s) along increasingly complex trajectories. The trajectories ranged from simple straight-line motion to complex sinusoidal paths, creating a dataset of around 80 million data points.

First, I tested all three architectures (LSTM, INN, PI/INN) on the traditional tasks that previous research had tackled. The results told a clear story: the LSTM remained the champion for location estimation, achieving errors of 9-18mm on simple paths and maintaining reasonable performance even on complex trajectories. The PI/INN showed promise, consistently outperforming the plain INN and demonstrating that physics constraints do help, especially on complex paths where the additional structure provides valuable guidance.

My research broke new ground when I tried extracting speed and volume characteristics. Using the primary estimates from each network, I then used these estimates to derive the characteristics analytically:

```python
def extract_speed(positions, timestamps):
    """
    Extract object speed from position estimates over time
    """
    # Compute displacement vectors
    displacements = positions[1:] - positions[:-1]
    time_deltas = timestamps[1:] - timestamps[:-1]
    
    # Instantaneous speeds
    speeds = np.linalg.norm(displacements, axis=1) / time_deltas
    
    # Average speed over trajectory (robust to noise)
    return np.mean(speeds)

def extract_volume(v_x, v_y, position, angle, speed, sensor_positions):
    """
    Extract sphere radius using inverse velocity potential
    """
    x, y = position
    best_estimates = []
    
    for i, sensor_pos in enumerate(sensor_positions):
        # Skip sensors with weak signals (low SNR)
        if abs(v_x[i]) < 1e-6:
            continue
            
        # Compute normalized position for this sensor
        rho = (sensor_pos - x) / y
        distance = np.sqrt(x**2 + y**2)
        
        # Get wavelet values
        psi_e, psi_o, psi_n = compute_wavelets(rho)
        
        # Expected velocity component based on wavelets
        expected_v_x = (psi_o * np.sin(angle) - psi_e * np.cos(angle))
        
        if abs(expected_v_x) > 1e-6:  # Avoid division by zero
            # Solve for radius: v_x = (a³||W||)/(2d³) × wavelet_combo
            radius_cubed = (2 * distance**3 * v_x[i]) / (speed * expected_v_x)
            radius = np.cbrt(abs(radius_cubed))
            best_estimates.append(radius)
    
    return np.median(best_estimates) if best_estimates else 0
```

The mathematics here reveals something about [error propagation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propagation_of_uncertainty). Since derived quantities depend on primary estimates, their accuracy is fundamentally limited by the underlying predictions. If our position estimates have error σₓ, our speed estimates will have error approximately:

$$
\sigma_\text{speed} \approx \sqrt{2} \times \frac{\sigma_x}{\Delta t}
$$

This means speed estimation is inherently more sensitive to noise than position estimation, explaining why good primary estimation is crucial for reliable secondary features.

The results showed that the LSTM achieved speed errors of 0.5-0.7 mm/s and volume errors of 0.2-1.9 mm , while the PI/INN showed moderate performance. For the first time, we could successfully extract object speed and volume from artificial lateral line sensor data – characteristics that had never been recovered before in this field.

Real-world sensors are noisy, so I tested how each architecture handled increasingly challenging conditions. Remarkably, in high-noise conditions, the physics-informed approach sometimes performed worse than the plain invertible network.

The reason illuminates an important principle in physics-informed learning. When noise is significant, physics constraints create competing objectives in the loss function:

$$
\text{Total Loss} = \text{Data Fitting Loss} + \lambda \times \text{Physics Loss}
$$

The data fitting term tries to match noisy observations, while the physics term enforces clean mathematical relationships. When noise is large, these objectives conflict – the noisy data no longer satisfies the physical constraints. The optimization process must choose between fitting the data and satisfying physics, often leading to suboptimal compromise solutions.

> ⚖️ **The Physics-Noise Trade-off**: When data is relatively clean, physics constraints are like having a wise teacher guide your learning. When data is noisy, those same constraints can become a stubborn teacher insisting on rules that don't match what you're seeing.

This can be seen mathematically. If our true signal follows the physics constraint $f(x,y) = 0$, but our noisy measurements satisfy $f(x,y) = \epsilon$ where $\epsilon$ is the noise contribution, then enforcing $f(x,y) = 0$ actually pulls us away from the best fit to the noisy data.

The LSTM's consistent superiority reinforced the critical importance of temporal context in hydrodynamic sensing. While individual velocity profiles theoretically contain complete information, temporal sequences provide noise averaging, consistency checking, and predictive context that dramatically improve accuracy. The temporal averaging effect follows:

$$
\text{Effective SNR} \approx \sqrt{N} \times \text{Single Measurement SNR}
$$

where N is the number of temporal observations. This square-root improvement with sample size is a key insight from statistics, and it explains why temporal models consistently outperform instantaneous approaches.

Physics-informed constraints help when conditions are favorable – clean data, well-understood physics, interpolation tasks – but they can hurt when data is noisy or models are imperfect. The key insight is that physics provides a valuable prior, but it shouldn't override strong contradictory evidence as the provided physical relations might not fit the situation.

Successfully extracting speed and volume revealed the importance of error propagation in derived quantities. The mathematical relationships show that errors in primary estimates (position, angle) propagate through the analytical derivations to affect secondary features. For the velocity potential inversion:

$$
\partial(\text{radius})/\partial(\text{velocity}) \propto \frac{1}{\text{wavelet amplitude}}
$$

This means errors are amplified when the wavelet amplitude is small, which happens when the object is far from sensors or moving in unfavorable orientations. Understanding these mathematical sensitivities helps explain why some configurations are much easier to characterize than others.

The successful extraction of novel features demonstrates that creative problem formulation can unlock new capabilities even with existing sensor technologies.

For more on this topic, I urge you to take a look at some of the work of Ben Wolf & Sietse van Netten. [For instance this paper.](https://pure.rug.nl/ws/portalfiles/portal/83201704/08706030.pdf)

Perhaps most importantly, this work demonstrates that nature remains a magnificent teacher. The solutions to tomorrow's technological challenges might already exist in the rivers, oceans, and streams around us, waiting for the right combination of biological curiosity, mathematical insight, and computational power to unlock their secrets.
